Best Finnish Dividend Stocks: Yield vs Coverage (2026)

Lauri Hynönen · 10 July 2026 · 8 min read

How the Finnish dividend stocks we cover rank

Two of the Finnish large-caps we cover, UPM and Kesko, lead our dividend list at a 4.9% forward yield in our June 2026 models. Both carry a BUY rating. That tie is where the useful part starts, because an identical yield can rest on very different foundations.

We have paired every yield with two coverage checks: the payout ratio, meaning how much of earnings goes out as dividend, and the free cash flow yield, meaning how much cash the business throws off relative to its market value. A high yield with thin cover is a dividend at risk. A high yield with fat cash cover is a dividend you can lean on.

Yield alone is misleading: check payout and free cash flow

UPM and Kesko both yield 4.9% in our models, but UPM covers that dividend far more comfortably. UPM's free cash flow yield is 9.8% in our 2026 estimate, against Kesko's 6.1%. Put plainly, UPM generates roughly twice the free cash, relative to its market value, that it needs to fund the payout. Kesko's cushion is real but thinner.

The payout ratio tells the same story from the earnings side. Our 2026 model has Kesko paying out about 87% of earnings as dividend, against 80% for UPM. Neither is reckless. But the closer a payout ratio sits to 100%, the less room a company has to hold the dividend through a bad year without dipping into the balance sheet or borrowing to pay shareholders.

This is why we rank on coverage, not yield. A 6% yield sitting on a 120% payout and negative free cash flow is a cut waiting to happen. A 4% yield covered twice over by cash is worth more than the headline suggests. Two identical yields are not the same dividend.

UPM is the best-covered 4.9% yield in our coverage

UPM is our top dividend pick in the covered set. It offers a 4.9% forward yield, the best cash cover on the list, and a BUY rating with a EUR 34.00 fair value against a EUR 25.31 share price when we published on 21 May 2026, about 34% of upside. The income case and the value case point the same way, which is unusual.

The assumption doing the work is that UPM's payout normalises. The company paid EUR 1.50 per share for 2025, and its dividend has run above reported earnings for three straight years in our model, with the payout above 160% in 2025 as pulp earnings troughed. UPM's stated policy, renewed in 2023, is to pay at least half of comparable earnings per share over time. Our model assumes earnings recover toward the existing dividend and pull the payout down to about 80% by 2026.

The risk is the mirror image of that assumption. If pulp prices stay weak and earnings do not recover, the 4.9% yield leans on a payout we already flag as high, and the EUR 1.50 gets harder to hold. We think the free cash flow supports it. We would be wrong if the pulp cycle stays at the bottom into 2027.

Kesko is a defensive 4.9% yield with a fuller payout

Kesko is the defensive alternative to UPM: the same 4.9% forward yield, but from a consumer-staples grocer rather than a pulp and paper cyclical. It is a BUY in our model, with a EUR 25.92 fair value against EUR 20.60 at our 5 June 2026 report, about 26% of upside. Grocery demand does not swing with the pulp cycle, so the earnings behind this dividend are steadier than UPM's.

The trade-off is coverage. Kesko's payout ratio is about 87% in our 2026 model, higher than UPM's 80%, and its free cash flow yield of 6.1% is lower. Kesko's own policy is to distribute 60 to 100% of comparable earnings per share, so an 87% payout sits inside plan rather than stretching it. The company proposed a EUR 0.90 dividend for 2025, paid in four instalments across the year, which suits an investor who wants income spread out rather than in one or two lumps.

Our read: Kesko for the stability of the payout, UPM for the size of the cash cushion behind it. Both are BUYs in our June 2026 models, and an income investor could hold both without much overlap in what drives the earnings.

Stora Enso, Nokia and Wärtsilä: yields we would not chase

The other three Finnish names we cover are not dividends we would buy for income today. Stora Enso shows a 4.0% forward yield in our model, but that figure is a forecast, not a record. The company paid just EUR 0.25 per share for 2025 and currently yields about 2.5%. Our 4.0% assumes the dividend recovers as Stora Enso returns to profit, and it assumes a payout near 80%, above the company's own stated policy of 50% of earnings over the cycle. Treat it as the least certain yield on the list. We still rate the share a BUY at a EUR 12.76 fair value versus EUR 10.05, but on the asset value, not the dividend. We look at both forest-products names together in our note on Finnish forestry stocks.

Nokia yields only 2.1%, and pays out about 94% of earnings to manage even that. We rate Nokia a SELL, with a EUR 11.50 fair value against a EUR 13.90 price, because the share is expensive on our numbers, not because of the payout. Wärtsilä yields the least at 1.4%, but on the most conservative payout on the list, about 40%. It has the most headroom to raise its dividend and, in our view, the least reason to own it now: we rate it a SELL at a EUR 30.00 fair value versus EUR 36.12. A low, well-covered yield can still sit on an expensive share.

What international investors keep after Finnish dividend tax

A non-resident individual pays up to 30% Finnish withholding tax on a dividend before it reaches the account, and a tax treaty usually cuts that to 15%. The gross yield is not what a foreign investor keeps. On a 4.9% gross yield, treaty-rate withholding of 15% leaves about 4.2%, while the full 30% domestic rate leaves about 3.4%. Corporate holders face a 20% standard rate.

The number to watch is the nominee-registered rate. When shares are held through a nominee, which is the normal setup for most foreign brokerage accounts, Finland withholds 35% unless the holding is documented for a lower treaty rate. Many brokers apply or reclaim the treaty rate for you, but not all do it automatically. Check how your broker handles Finnish withholding before you buy a Helsinki name for income, because a 35% haircut turns a 4.9% gross yield into roughly 3.2% net. On dividends, the tax wrapper can matter as much as the yield you started from.

Frequently asked questions

What are the highest-yielding Finnish dividend stocks we cover?

Among the Finnish large-caps we cover, UPM and Kesko lead at a 4.9% forward dividend yield in our June 2026 models, followed by Stora Enso at 4.0% and Fortum at 3.6%. Once payout coverage is added we rank UPM first, because its 9.8% free cash flow yield backs the dividend more comfortably than any peer on the list.

Is UPM a good dividend stock?

In our view, yes, on both income and value. UPM yields 4.9% in our 2026 model, covers the payout with a 9.8% free cash flow yield, and traded about 34% below our EUR 34.00 fair value as of 21 May 2026. The main risk is a prolonged weak pulp cycle that delays the earnings recovery the payout assumes.

How safe is Stora Enso's dividend?

Less certain than the headline yield suggests. Stora Enso paid EUR 0.25 per share for 2025 and currently yields about 2.5%. Our 4.0% forward yield assumes the dividend recovers as the company returns to profit, on a payout near 80% that is above its stated 50%-of-earnings policy. We rate the share a BUY on asset value, not on the dividend.

How much tax do foreign investors pay on Finnish dividends?

A non-resident individual faces up to 30% Finnish withholding tax, usually reduced to 15% under a tax treaty. Shares held through a nominee are withheld at 35% unless documented for a lower rate, and corporate holders face 20%. On a 4.9% gross yield, treaty-rate withholding leaves about 4.2% net.

Do Finnish dividend stocks pay dividends quarterly?

Not usually. Most pay once or twice a year. Kesko is the exception among the names we cover, splitting its EUR 0.90 dividend for 2025 into four instalments across the year. UPM and Stora Enso pay in two instalments, typically around spring and autumn. Quarterly payers are rare on Nasdaq Helsinki.
