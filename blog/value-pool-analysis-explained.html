Value Pool Analysis for Stocks: Tesla vs UPM Example

Lauri Hynönen · 19 June 2026 · 7 min read

What is value pool analysis?

Value pool analysis splits a company's enterprise value into the separate businesses, or pools, that the market is paying for, then tests whether each pool's economics can support the value assigned to it. It is a form of sum-of-the-parts valuation, the standard approach for multi-business companies where one blended P/E or EV/EBITDA hides more than it shows (Corporate Finance Institute).

The point is to find where the value actually sits. A single multiple on a diversified company tells you nothing about which division carries the price. Break the same enterprise value into pools and the question sharpens: is the market paying for the cash the company earns today, or for a business that does not exist yet?

We build a value pool map for every company we cover. Each pool gets its own revenue, its own margin, and the share of enterprise value the market implies for it. The two examples below, Tesla and UPM, run the same technique to opposite conclusions.

Value pool analysis vs a single valuation multiple

A single multiple values the whole company at one number. Value pool analysis values each business and adds them up. For a focused company the two agree. For a company that staples a cash business to a speculative one, they diverge hard.

Take a stock trading at 30x EV/EBITDA. That multiple could mean the market expects steady growth across the whole firm. Or it could mean one mature division is priced at 8x and a pre-revenue division is carrying the rest. Those are different investments with different risks, and the blended multiple cannot tell them apart. The pool split can.

So we treat value pools as a layer on top of a standard valuation, not a replacement for it. A DCF or a peer multiple still sets the number for each pool. The pool map tells you which assumptions that number rests on, and which pool breaks the thesis if it disappoints. It pairs naturally with reverse valuation: once you know a pool's implied value, you can solve for the volumes and margins it quietly assumes.

Tesla value pool analysis: 79.6% of the price is pre-revenue

In our 1 June 2026 model, about 79.6% of Tesla's USD 1,573.2 billion enterprise value sits in three businesses that earned no revenue in 2025: Full Self-Driving software, the Robotaxi network, and the Optimus robot. The two pools that actually generate cash, Core Automotive and Energy, carry just 20.4% of the price.

Tesla reports two segments to the SEC, automotive and energy generation and storage (Form 10-K). The FSD, Robotaxi, and Optimus pools are our analytical split of the optionality the market is paying for, not lines in Tesla's accounts. That distinction is the whole point: there is no reported profit to value in those three pools, so the roughly USD 1.25 trillion they carry rests entirely on what they might earn later.

The reverse valuation makes the gap concrete. We value Tesla's two cash pools on a 15x EBIT multiple, which supports only about USD 110 billion of enterprise value, against the USD 1.57 trillion the market assigns. The USD 549.7 billion in the FSD pool implies roughly 44 million subscribers at that multiple. Tesla had about 1.28 million active FSD subscribers in Q1 2026. We rate the stock SELL with a USD 350.00 target against a USD 438.26 price, and the pool map is why.

UPM value pool analysis: half the price is a tangible asset floor

Value pool analysis reaches the opposite verdict on UPM-Kymmene. In our 21 May 2026 model, roughly half of UPM's EUR 16.4 billion enterprise value, about EUR 8 to 9 billion, is anchored by two tangible pools: UPM Energy, which runs Finnish hydropower and a stake in the Olkiluoto nuclear plant, plus the company's owned forestry land. Add Advanced Materials at EUR 3 to 3.5 billion and EUR 11 to 12.5 billion of the price is covered by businesses that already earn their keep.

UPM reports six business areas, among them UPM Fibres, UPM Energy, and UPM Raflatac (UPM Annual Report 2024). Our value pools group them by what drives their value: a tangible floor, a stable compounding core, and a cyclical pulp-plus-options residual. What is left after the floor and Advanced Materials, the entire pulp portfolio and the Leuna biochemicals plant, is in the market for EUR 3 to 5 billion.

We think that residual is too cheap, which is why we rate UPM BUY with a EUR 34.00 target against a EUR 25.31 price. The pulp business alone runs 5.8 million tonnes of capacity at a first-quartile Uruguayan cash cost near USD 280 per tonne. We assume a mid-cycle pulp price of USD 650 per tonne, and on that assumption price normalisation closes a EUR 900 million EBITDA gap in Fibres with no volume growth required. How to run a value pool analysis on a stock

You can run a value pool analysis in five steps, and a rough version works from public filings before any model.

  1. List the businesses. Start from the company's reported segments, then split any segment where a profitable operation and a speculative one are reported together. Tesla's automotive line buries the FSD software economics, so separate them.
  2. Value each pool on its own merits. Use a peer multiple or a DCF suited to that business. A regulated power asset and a software option do not share a multiple.
  3. Add the pools to an enterprise value, then subtract net debt for equity value. This is the sum-of-the-parts step (Damodaran, NYU Stern).
  4. Compare the total to the market price. The gap tells you which pool the market disagrees with you about.
  5. Pressure-test the largest pool. Solve for the revenue or volume it implies, the way our reverse valuation does, and ask whether that number is reachable.

The discipline is in step one. Most of the mispricing we find comes from a cash business and an option being valued as if they were the same thing. Our methodology page sets out how we build the pool map for every covered name.

Where value pool analysis goes wrong

The weakness of value pool analysis is that the pool boundaries and the multiple on each pool are judgment calls, and both swing the answer. Draw Tesla's pools differently, or grant the FSD pool a richer multiple, and the SELL case softens. The technique is only as honest as its assumptions, so we state ours and show the scenario math instead of a single number.

Our two calls can be wrong in specific ways. If Tesla turns FSD into a mandatory autonomous-driving subscription and scales it, the USD 549.7 billion pool stops being speculative and our SELL is wrong. On UPM, if pulp prices settle durably below USD 500 per tonne, the Paso de los Toros mill does not clear its cost of capital and the tangible-floor argument weakens. Both are the first things we watch.

What value pool analysis will not give you is a view on management or a read on a genuinely new situation. It is an accounting of where value sits, not a forecast of who executes. Pair it with the qualitative work and it tells you which question actually decides the stock.

Frequently asked questions

What is value pool analysis?

Value pool analysis splits a company's enterprise value into the distinct businesses, or pools, that the market is paying for, then checks whether each pool's revenue and margins can support its share of the price. It is a practical form of sum-of-the-parts valuation used for multi-business companies.

How is value pool analysis different from sum-of-the-parts?

It is the same idea applied with a sharper question. Sum-of-the-parts adds up segment values to find a total. Value pool analysis goes further by testing what each pool's price implies, then asking which pool the market has mispriced and which one breaks the investment thesis if it disappoints.

What does Tesla's value pool analysis show?

In our 1 June 2026 model, about 79.6% of Tesla's USD 1,573.2 billion enterprise value sits in FSD, Robotaxi, and Optimus, three pools with no 2025 revenue. The cash businesses support only around USD 110 billion. That split underpins our SELL rating and USD 350.00 target.

Is value pool analysis useful for ordinary single-business stocks?

Less so. For a focused company, a value pool split and a single multiple give almost the same answer. The technique earns its keep on conglomerates, holding companies, and optionality-heavy stocks where one division carries the price and a blended multiple hides which one.
