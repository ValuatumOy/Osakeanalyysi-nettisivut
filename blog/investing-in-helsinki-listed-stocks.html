How to invest in Finnish stocks: Nasdaq Helsinki guide

Lauri Hynönen · 3 July 2026 · 9 min read

How do you invest in Finnish stocks?

You invest in Finnish stocks the same way you buy any foreign share: through a broker that offers access to Nasdaq Helsinki, the exchange where Finnish equities trade. Around 140 companies list there, every one priced in euros, and the order book runs on weekdays from 10:00 to 18:30 Helsinki time.

The practical steps are short. Open an account with a broker that routes orders to Helsinki (most large international brokers do). Fund it in euros, or accept that your platform converts from your home currency at the point of trade. Place the order by ticker: Nokia is NOKIA.HE, UPM is UPM.HE. Your shares are almost always held in a nominee, or street-name, account, which matters for how your dividends get taxed. We cover that below.

You do not need to live in Finland or hold a Finnish account. What you do need to watch is the euro. If you report in dollars or pounds, your return is the stock's move plus or minus the currency.

What is Nasdaq Helsinki?

Nasdaq Helsinki is Finland's only regulated stock exchange, home to roughly 140 listed companies and run by Nasdaq as part of its Nordic markets. It was the Helsinki Stock Exchange for most of its life, passed to OMX in the 2000s, and became part of Nasdaq when Nasdaq bought OMX in 2008. Trading and reporting are in euros.

The benchmark is the OMX Helsinki 25 (OMXH25), which tracks the 25 most-traded stocks on the exchange. It is capitalisation-weighted, with any single company capped at 10% so that one name cannot dominate the index. That cap tells you something about the market: without it, a handful of large caps would swamp everything else. Helsinki is a small, concentrated market by US standards, and a few companies carry most of the trading volume and most of the index.

Which companies and sectors dominate Helsinki?

Four sectors carry most of the Helsinki market's weight: communication equipment, industrials, forest products, and consumer staples. The names below are five of the largest, and the five we publish full valuations on. Forestry is the sector that makes Helsinki distinctive. UPM and Stora Enso each own vast tracts of Nordic forest land, so their share prices are part industrial cash flow and part biological land bank.

How are Finnish dividends and gains taxed for foreign investors?

Finland withholds tax at source on dividends before they reach a non-resident investor. According to the Finnish Tax Administration (Vero), the standard rate for a non-resident individual is 30%, and dividends on nominee-registered shares whose beneficiary the payer cannot identify are taxed at 35%. For a corporate beneficiary the rate is 20%.

A tax treaty between Finland and your country of residence usually cuts that rate, commonly to 15% for individuals, but only if you supply the identification and residence documents the payer needs. Since 2021 this runs through the OECD TRACE procedure for nominee-registered listed shares. If too much was withheld, you reclaim the difference from Vero. This is the part most foreign investors get wrong: without correct paperwork you can face the full 30% or 35% and have to claim the rest back later.

Capital gains on listed Finnish shares are generally taxable in your country of residence rather than in Finland, but that too depends on your treaty. None of this is tax advice. Confirm your own position with the tax authority where you live before you buy.

What are the risks of investing in Helsinki-listed stocks?

The first risk is the one many foreign investors underweight: currency. Everything on Nasdaq Helsinki is priced in euros, so a strengthening home currency can erase a euro gain. The second is concentration. This is a small market where the OMXH25 leans on a few large caps, and liquidity in mid-caps is thinner than anything you see in US names. The third is single-name cyclicality. Forestry earnings swing with pulp prices, and industrials swing with the global capex cycle.

The risk to our own view is more specific. Both of our sell ratings, Nokia and Wärtsilä, rest on treating today's AI and data-centre demand as a cyclical peak. If hyperscaler demand for optical networking and off-grid power turns out to be structural and durable, both sells are wrong and the market's premium is the correct price, not an overpayment. That is the single assumption most likely to sink our Helsinki calls, and it is worth watching the hyperscaler order data before you lean on either rating.

Frequently asked questions

Can foreign investors buy Finnish stocks?

Yes. Any investor can buy Finnish shares through a broker that offers access to Nasdaq Helsinki, where about 140 companies trade in euros. You do not need to be resident in Finland. Shares are usually held in a nominee account, which affects how your dividends are taxed at source.

How are Finnish dividends taxed for non-residents?

The Finnish Tax Administration withholds 30% on dividends to a non-resident individual, or 35% on nominee-registered shares where the beneficiary is unknown. A tax treaty commonly reduces this to 15% if you file the right residence and identity documents; otherwise you reclaim the excess from Vero afterwards.

What is the main Finnish stock index?

The OMX Helsinki 25 (OMXH25) is the benchmark. It tracks the 25 most-traded stocks on Nasdaq Helsinki, weighted by market capitalisation with any single company capped at 10% so that one large name cannot dominate the index. Nokia, UPM and Kesko are among its constituents.

Is Helsinki a good market for dividend investors?

Several large Helsinki names pay well. In our June 2026 models UPM and Kesko both yield about 4.9% and Stora Enso about 4.0%. The AI-repriced names yield less: Nokia 2.1% and Wärtsilä 1.4%. So the higher-yielding stocks are the materials and staples, not the momentum names.
