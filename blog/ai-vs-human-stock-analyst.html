AI vs Human Stock Analyst: Who Actually Says Sell

Lauri Hynönen · 9 August 2026 · 6 min read

AI vs human stock analyst: the gap shows up in the sell ratings

Across the seven companies where both we and the sell-side publish a rating, the street has 396 individual ratings on the books: 223 buys, 141 holds, 32 sells. Sells are 8.1% of the total. Our model is negative on three of those same seven names.

That gap is most of the story, and it has little to do with intelligence. A sell rating is expensive for a person to publish. It costs management access, it strains the banking relationship the research desk sits next to, and being bearish and wrong on your own is worse for a career than being bullish and wrong alongside everyone else. A model carries none of that. It compares a 12-month target to a share price and reports the sign.

We are not claiming our three negative calls are right. We are claiming they were cheap for us to publish and expensive for anyone with a client list. Those are different things, and only the second one is structural.

The mechanism is dull on purpose. Take Nokia. Our 15 July 2026 model builds a base case on EUR 21,981m of revenue, EUR 3,787m of EBITDA and a 14.8x EV/EBITDA multiple, which bridges to EUR 10.08 per share against a EUR 10.28 price. The bear case, at a 15.8% margin and 12.1x, gives EUR 7.20. A base case that lands 1.8% below the market price and a bear case 30% below it produces a negative rating, and the model does not get a vote on whether that is awkward.

The assumption doing the work is the 17.2% group EBITDA margin in the base case. Push it toward the bull case at 19.2% and fair value goes to EUR 12.22, which is a different article entirely. That number is the one to argue with.

One clean hit, one clean miss, and no proof either way

Nokia has fallen 20.7% since our July report, from EUR 10.28 to EUR 8.156 on 9 August 2026, while the street's grade counts still lean 26 buys to 4 sells. That is the case for the machine, and it is the example we would pick if we were selling you something.

So here is the other one. Our 7 July 2026 Kesko model set a EUR 17.10 target against a EUR 19.20 price and rated the stock a SELL. Kesko traded at EUR 21.46 on 9 August 2026. The call is 11.8% offside in five weeks, and the reason is exactly the weakness you would expect from a model: it read a grocery margin as mean-reverting when the market was reading it as a step change.

Fifteen rated companies over a few weeks is not evidence. It is a sample small enough that one Finnish grocer moves the whole record. We would need three or four years of dated calls before the hit rate meant anything, and we will publish that record when it exists rather than a curated version of it now.

Where a human analyst still beats the model

On any question whose answer is a judgment about people, the human wins, and Intel is the clearest live example. Our 5 June 2026 model put a USD 98.00 target on a USD 114.68 price, working from a 106.4x forward P/E and a 0.4% free cash flow yield. Consensus sits at USD 110.41. The range behind that consensus runs from USD 60 to USD 200.

That spread is not sloppiness. It is the right response to a business whose value turns on whether a named external customer commits volume to the 18A node, and on whether the people promising it are credible. Our model cannot weigh a chief executive's record on hitting a process milestone. An analyst who has sat through fifteen years of those calls can.

So the honest comparison is narrow. The model is better at the arithmetic and worse at the character. Our higher sell count is not evidence that we see more risk than the street does. Nothing stops us from writing it down, that is all.

Ask either side for the same two things: the assumption carrying the valuation, and the fact that would break it. Our Nokia thesis breaks on a clean Infinera integration delivering 15% optical margins with sustained 800G hyperscaler orders. A rating that cannot name its own breaker is a vote, not a view.

Frequently asked questions

Is an AI stock analyst better than a human analyst?

On arithmetic, yes. AI applies one valuation method identically across a coverage list and has no relationship or career cost attached to a negative rating, which is why our sell share is 26.7% of 15 rated companies against 8.1% of the 396 sell-side ratings on our overlapping names. On judging management credibility, humans are still better.

Why do so few analysts issue sell ratings?

Cost, not conviction. A negative rating can end management access, sits uncomfortably next to an investment banking relationship, and carries more career risk than agreeing with consensus and being wrong together. Across the seven companies we also cover, 32 of 396 published grades are sells, and two of the seven have none at all.

Can AI replace equity research analysts?

Not the whole job. AI is stronger on structured work: consistent multiples, scenario bridges, full coverage of filings without triage. It is weaker on genuinely novel situations and on judging whether a management team's stated plan is credible, which is where Intel's USD 60 to USD 200 target range actually comes from.

How do I check whether a rating is any good?

Ask for the assumption that carries the valuation and the fact that would break it. Our Nokia base case rests on a 17.2% EBITDA margin at 14.8x EV/EBITDA; move that margin to 19.2% and fair value goes from EUR 10.08 to EUR 12.22 per share. If a rating cannot name the number it depends on, treat it as an opinion.
