Valuation Methodology

How to value a stock: DCF, multiples, and reverse valuation

Lauri Hynönen · 22 June 2026 · 7 min read

What stock valuation actually asks

Stock valuation asks one question: what should this company be worth, given what it will earn? The catch is that "what it will earn" is a forecast, and small changes in that forecast can halve or double the implied value of the same stock.

Three methods answer the question differently. Price multiples compare a company to its peers or to its own history. Discounted cash flow (DCF) converts projected future earnings into today's money. Reverse valuation works backwards from the current share price to see what assumptions are already embedded in the price. Each method has a weakness. Used together, they narrow the range.

A number to orient the discussion: Tesla traded at a P/E of 239.4x in our June 2026 model. UPM-Kymmene traded at 16.4x. Both companies are profitable. Neither multiple is obviously right or wrong without knowing what each business is being paid to become.

Method 1: price multiples

P/E divides share price by earnings per share. It tells you how many years of current earnings you are paying for. But P/E breaks down when earnings are temporarily depressed, when a company is pre-earnings, or when the market is pricing in a business that does not yet exist.

EV/EBITDA (enterprise value divided by EBITDA) is generally more useful for capital-intensive companies. It strips out financing and tax differences, allowing fairer comparison across different capital structures. FCF yield (free cash flow divided by market capitalisation) tells you what you are actually receiving in cash, which is often different from what the income statement implies.

From our June 2026 coverage:

Tesla (TSLA)239.4x85.2x−0.1%SELL
Nokia (NOKIA.HE)43.6x19.7x3.2%SELL
UPM-Kymmene (UPM.HE)16.4x7.8x9.8%BUY

UPM at 7.8x EV/EBITDA is cheap against global paper and pulp peers at 9-11x. Nokia at 19.7x is expensive for a hardware business in a mature cycle, but the market is pricing an optical networking growth story. Tesla at 85.2x is pricing something that has not yet generated revenue.

The critical discipline: compare to the right peer group. A 20x P/E is expensive for a utility and cheap for a high-growth software company. The multiple only signals value when the comparison group is correct.

P/E and EV/EBITDA can disagree on the same stock, and sometimes point opposite ways. We work through when to trust which in our guide to EV/EBITDA vs the P/E ratio.

Method 2: discounted cash flow

A DCF values a business by summing all future free cash flows, each discounted back to today at a rate reflecting the riskiness of those cash flows. You need three inputs: a free cash flow forecast over an explicit period (typically 5-10 years), a discount rate (usually WACC, the weighted average cost of capital), and a terminal value for everything beyond the forecast period.

The terminal value is where most of the risk sits. In a 10-year DCF, terminal value typically accounts for 60-75% of the total output. A single percentage point change in the terminal growth rate assumption can move the result by 15-20%. When analysts argue about a DCF, they are almost always arguing about the terminal assumption, not the near-term forecast.

UPM is a DCF-friendly company. Capital expenditure falls from EUR 715 million in 2024 to roughly EUR 300 million by 2026, converting approximately EUR 1.7 billion of EBITDA into over EUR 687 million of free cash flow, with no volume growth required. That cash flow is largely predictable and can be modelled with reasonable confidence.

Tesla is the opposite case. Projected free cash flow in 2026 is negative USD 1.74 billion, driven by a USD 19.4 billion capital expenditure program funding the transition to software and logistics revenue. A DCF of Tesla rests almost entirely on terminal assumptions about margins and multiples 10+ years out. Our base case uses a USD 102.3 billion revenue base with an 18.1% EBITDA margin at an 85.2x EV/EBITDA multiple, producing a fair value of USD 421.03 per share. Change the multiple to 21.6x and the output falls to USD 108.42. That sensitivity is why the third method matters.

Method 3: reverse valuation

Reverse valuation starts from the current share price and asks: what assumptions about revenue, margin, and multiples must be true for today's price to be justified? If those assumptions are implausible, the stock is expensive regardless of the narrative. If they are conservative, the stock is cheap.

For Tesla at USD 438.26 (June 2026), our model assigns USD 549.7 billion of enterprise value to the FSD software and autonomous driving business. Justifying that allocation at a 15x EBIT multiple requires roughly USD 36.6 billion of recurring operating profit from FSD, equivalent to 44 million active subscribers. At the time of the report, Tesla had approximately 1.28 million active FSD subscribers at $99 per month.

The bear case compression scenario, where EV/EBITDA contracts from 85.2x to 21.6x, produces a Tesla share price of USD 108.42, representing 75% downside from USD 438.26. That is not a tail risk. It is the price if autonomous software fails to scale.

For UPM, the bear case requires global pulp prices to settle durably below $500 per tonne, preventing the EUR 3 billion Paso de los Toros mill from clearing its cost of capital. That produces a bear-case value of EUR 8.74 per share against a current price of EUR 25.31. Possible; not our base expectation.

Reverse valuation answers the question the other two methods do not easily address: what exactly has to go wrong for you to lose serious money? The full Tesla reverse valuation and the UPM analysis are available free.

When the three methods give different answers

They often do. Tesla at USD 438.26 is roughly fairly valued on a base-case DCF (USD 421.03 per share), expensive on every multiple (P/E 239x, EV/EBITDA 85.2x), and potentially catastrophic in the bear case (USD 108.42 per share). The methods disagree because they are measuring different things.

The DCF base case and the multiples analysis disagree on Tesla because the DCF is embedding the successful-software outcome in its terminal assumptions, while the multiples comparison is against current hardware peers. Both are internally consistent. They imply different beliefs about which business Tesla primarily is.

Our view: the option on autonomous software is real. But the current embedded assumptions require a scale, specifically 44 million FSD subscribers and a deployed Robotaxi fleet of 2.1 million vehicles, that is mathematically difficult to achieve with a hardware fleet producing fewer than 2 million vehicles annually. We rate Tesla SELL at a USD 350.00 target.

The general rule: when DCF and multiples analyses disagree sharply, the gap almost always sits in the terminal value assumption or the exit multiple used to terminate the DCF. Reverse valuation makes that assumption explicit and testable rather than hidden inside a long-range forecast.

Valuing stocks with no earnings

Pre-profit companies require different metrics. P/E is undefined; DCF relies on terminal assumptions that are already sensitive enough for mature businesses, and far more speculative for early-stage ones.

EV/Revenue applies when EBITDA is negative. For a SaaS company at 4x EV/Revenue, you are paying four years of current revenue. Whether that is expensive depends on revenue growth and gross margin, not on net income. Sector context matters: 4x is cheap for software growing 40% annually, and expensive for a slow-growth industrial.

EV/EBITDA applies as soon as the company reaches positive EBITDA, even if net income remains negative. It removes the distortions from depreciation and financing costs that make P/E unreliable for capital-intensive businesses.

For high-growth software businesses, the Rule of 40 is commonly used: revenue growth rate plus EBITDA margin should exceed 40. A company growing 60% with a -10% EBITDA margin passes; a company growing 15% with 10% EBITDA margin barely passes. It captures the growth-profitability trade-off without requiring a full DCF.

No single metric works reliably for pre-profit companies. Multiple imperfect measures used alongside a reverse valuation stress test provides more information than any one ratio in isolation.

Where the numbers come from

Annual and quarterly financial statements are the primary source for any fundamental valuation. US public companies file 10-K annual reports and 10-Q quarterly updates with the SEC; the EDGAR full-text search at sec.gov is free and covers the full filing history of every listed US company. Helsinki-listed companies file with the Finnish Financial Supervisory Authority and on the Nasdaq Helsinki exchange site.

FINRA's investor resources at finra.org include practical guidance on reading company disclosures and on recognising when a stock is being promoted rather than genuinely undervalued. The SEC's investor education site at investor.gov covers how to read a 10-K and what to look for in the financial notes.

For comparable multiples and historical data, Macrotrends.net and Yahoo Finance offer free historical P/E and EV/EBITDA data. Bloomberg and FactSet provide cleaner institutional-grade screens but require subscriptions.

A principle worth taking from Prof. Aswath Damodaran's valuation work at NYU Stern, the most widely cited public reference on the subject: the model is only as good as the inputs. Running a DCF on consensus analyst forecasts reproduces consensus thinking. The information gain comes from identifying where your estimate of long-run margins or growth differs from the market's embedded assumption, and having a specific reason for that difference. Frequently asked questions

What is a good P/E ratio for a stock?

There is no universal answer. P/E must be compared to the right reference group: sector peers, the company's own history, or the broad market. A P/E of 15x is average for a mature industrial company, cheap for a high-growth software company, and expensive for a low-growth utility. In our June 2026 coverage, UPM trades at 16.4x P/E (BUY) and Tesla at 239.4x (SELL). Both are informative only in context.

What is reverse valuation in stock analysis?

Reverse valuation starts from the current share price and works backwards to identify what revenue, margin, and multiple assumptions are already embedded in that price. If those assumptions require implausible outcomes (44 million FSD subscribers for Tesla, for example), the stock is overvalued. If they are conservative, it may be cheap. It answers the question: what has to go right, or wrong, from here? Our free reports on Tesla and UPM include the full reverse-valuation scenarios.

How do you value a stock with no earnings?

Use EV/Revenue when EBITDA is negative, EV/EBITDA as soon as the business turns positive EBITDA even if net income remains negative, and the Rule of 40 (revenue growth + EBITDA margin > 40) for high-growth software businesses. A reverse valuation stress test on the implied revenue scale and margin required to justify the current price is often more informative than any single ratio.

What is the difference between DCF and price multiples?

DCF values a company on its own projected future cash flows, discounted to today. It is absolute and company-specific. Price multiples compare a company to peers or history: P/E, EV/EBITDA, FCF yield. They are relative and dependent on the reference group being appropriate. DCF is most reliable for mature, predictable businesses; multiples are useful for quick comparisons across sectors. The two methods often disagree, and reconciling the disagreement is where the analytical judgment lies.

How do you calculate the intrinsic value of a stock?

The standard method is discounted cash flow: project free cash flows over 5-10 years, add a terminal value (typically the largest component), and discount everything at the WACC. The output is an intrinsic value per share. The challenge is that small changes in the terminal growth rate or discount rate produce large changes in the output. Checking the result against comparable multiples and running a reverse valuation to see what the market is already pricing in gives more confidence in the range than any single DCF output alone.
